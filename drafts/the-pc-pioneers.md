# The 9 True Pioneers of the PC
We have all heard the story that Jobs and Gates are responsible for the personal computer revolution. Maybe you have even heard of Gate's partner Paul Allen or Job's partner Steve Wozniak, who arguably 
made larger contributions (especially the former). There is no denying that without these guys, the personal computer would have been different -- but I surmise it still would exist. At the time they 
were hacking hardware and installing software, there were already dozens (if not hundreds) of hobbiests doing the same, they were not in anyway unique, they were just better business people and faster -- 
able to get the first to market.

To me, being lucky, opportunistic, and good at business does not make you a pioneer. Being a pioneer means you truly come up with a revolutionary idea, you lay a foundation upon which everything else 
is built. Even the iPhone, which arguably may be jobs lasting legacy, was not something he pioneered, he actually argued against turning the iPod into a phone saying

> Why the f*** would we want that? That is the dumbest idea I've ever heard.

Another problem is that many technologies we have came from corporations, or corporate funded labs like Bell Labs where ideas and innovators are hard to truly identify since it is all part of a shared team.

Here we root out teams, and only list people whose *sole* contributions were vital to PC-creation and get to the root of the true pioneers of the PC.

## Charles Babbage, *1820-1840*
*The first to conceive of a general purpose machine with memory, a processor, and conditionals*

Until Charles Babbage came along computers were seen as specific tools like a shovel, in otherwords, not really computers at all (in any sense of the modern word). They were hardcoded devices, driven mechanically by gears. One of the more sophisticated computers of this time was the arithmometer, invented right around the time Charles was making his own innovations. The pascaline no doubt had some influence on Charles work (created by Blaise Pascal) which was invented in the 17th century, but was extremely primitive -- nothing more than a counting device.

## Ada Lovelace, *1843*
*The first to envision that a general purpose machine does not need to be limited to mathematics* 

Ada, the child of Lord Byron (a famous poet who hung out in circles with Mary, the author of Frankenstein, and Percy Shelley) was driven into mathematics by her mother so that she would not inherit Byron's volatile tempermenant. Ada's mentor in mathematics was Charles Babbage, and she became involved in Babbage's Analytical Engine work. She is believed to have sketched out the first algorithm in her own notes, some even calling her "the first programmer."

More importantly, Ada realized that Babbage's machine (and computers in general) may some day not just be a mathematical calculators, but capable of symbolic manipulation, allowing more than just the discipline of mathematics to be resolved, but instead outside domains like music.

Ada likely would have been forgotten by history if her musings had not had an impact on Alan Turning who discovered her papers while studying Babbage's work, further enshrining her in history as Alan Turing is arguable the most important person on this entire list.

## Claude Shannon, *1937-1948*
*Showed how bits and binary could be used to encode information -- the final pathway to electronic computers*

Shannon's paper "A Mathematical Theory of Communication" established that all information could be quantified as binary bits. This paired with three others at Bell Labs (William Shockley, John Bardeen, Walter Brattain) who create the transistor set the hardware groundwork for the computer. Only Shannon is listed here since his work is creditable to a singular individual.  Shannon also worked on signal noise theory along with another researcher (Ralph Hartley), showing how the maximum output can be computed relative to noise.

## Grace Hopper, *1952-1959*
*The first compiler and the first to champion readable code -- forsight that computers would extend to everyone*

Grace Hopper was widely mocked by her contemporaries for pushing the idea of high level programming languages (instead of assembly / machine code), at a time when technology made such a notion impractical if not impossible. She pushed forward and became the first person to develop a compiler. Grace went on to develop COBOL from her orignal FLOMATIC concept. She leaned even more heavily into human readability, to such an extent that some programmers would argue actually made the language harder to write as it made it extremely verbose. Yet, even today, nearly a quarter of a century after her death, COBOL is still running on many financial systems cementing her legacy as someone who saw high level languages as the predominant way to program. Estimates are that now more than 99+% of all code written is in high-level-languages by the original definition, something that would have been absurd to her contemporaries.

In his memoirs, Gary even suggests Grace may have been one of the first to take microprocessors seriously, something he pursued (as we will discuss)

>She gazed sneakily at me with those eyes that shown in that brilliance of hers, though her outward appearance was of frail demeanor. With stealth, Grace reached into her purse and brought out an Intel 4004 chip, then whispered to me "this is the future."

## Gary Kildall, *1972-1991*
*The first to prove a PC was actually possibly by developing the 1st viable OS for a microprocessor*

While Gary Kildall may not be the singular most important person in the history of computers (that likely goes to someone like Turing or Shannon), he very likely is the singular most important person in the history of *Personal* computers. Gary Kildall proved that PCs were viable before any computer clubs even toyed with the idea. He was one the first (possibly *the* first) to even think that a microprocessor could run and compile high level programming languages AND also one of the first to imagine they could run an operating system, furthermore, he not only thought it, he was the first to demonstrate both in 1973 and again in 1974. This was before Bill Gates was booting up a basic BASIC screen or Wozniak was toying with the Apple 1 -- well before all of these guys Kildall had a fully functioning operating system, his own programming language and compiler, and he had demonstrated and begun selling it. Furthermore, some of them, like Gates even developed applications for CP/M, which was a common past time at the computer clubs.

Even more interesting is that Gary continued his innovations, working on GEM (a graphical user interface around the time of the original Macintosh), he was one of the first pioneers of the CD, and was the first creator of a digital encyclopedia which also employed hyperlinks -- the first commercial use of that technology. In fact, Gary is also credited as the inventor of the BIO, as he needed to boot CP/M on different hardware (something not conceived of at the time he made CP/M as everything was mainframes).

So it is sad then that all these years later, no one knows of Gary Kildall. The truth is, Gary was, by all accounts, simply too nice. He was open with technological ideas, not trying to make a quick buck, and this likely led partially to why he wasn't willing to let IBM do whatever it wanted to his company (which is also what gave MSDOS its rise to dominance). But many people frame it as a bad business decision, no -- it was just a good human one. Gary cared about his employees and was a legitimate nerd, he cared more about that then money -- eventhough he ended up making a decent amount, he never achieved the fame or wealth of the others, and it is likely because of the constant pestering of him and reminders of his supposed failure that he turned to alcohol in his later years and ultimately died, something his old friend Stewart Chefeit continued to insist on multiple interviews up until his own death late last year. 

Perhaps nothing sums up the unfair treatment of Gary better than this:

> In 1992, the University of Washington invited Kildall to attend the 25th anniversary of its computer science program. He was one of its earliest and most distinguished graduates, having earned a PhD there. They didn't ask him to address the audience despite his renown as a graduate. Instead they chose Bill Gates — who had dropped out of Harvard and never attended the University of Washington — to give the keynote, apparently because he had donated money. 

What makes this even worse is that MS-DOS is arguably a rip-off of CP/M having been purchased from a man who knowing cloned CP/M's API from the original manual, so the entire reason Bill even had the money for that donation in the first place was because he (philosophically, if not legally) "stole" MS-DOS from Gary who only got an invite to be part of the audience and listen to Bill's speech.

## Ed Roberts *1975*
*The first to see PC hardware as being economically producable for a home user*

Ed Roberts is arguably the weakest addition on this list, he does fall a bit under the lucky category, similar to that of Jobs, Gates, and others. However, he was truly the first to realize an economical PC was possible -- even if his vision was not as grandoise as Gary Kildall. At first, he saw the "PC" as a toy that could be manufactured to save his company that had sold rocket telemetry kits and calculators. When his company began to struggle due to a crash in calculator sales, he was forced to come up with new ideas, one of them was to sell a computing device with switches mounted on the front. It was a personal computer called the Altair 8800, and although he didn't think that much of it at the time, the sales exploded, and the accessibly cheap computer combined with Gary Kildalls CP/M and Gary's vision of microprocessors as powerful PCs, set the entire foundation upon which Wozniak, Gates, Allen, Jobs, and the computer club stood to start their hacking and own PC development. 

## Richard Stallman (1983-1992)
*The first to champion software controlled by users (GPL/Open Source) -- and a pioneer in early software that made modern software possible*

Richard Stallman, while at times somewhat controversial, is nonetheless essential to mention. Unlike his contemporaries, Stallman was content collecting a meager stipend as a research hire, developing software at MIT's AI Laboratory. He would sometimes sleep in his office, and professed that he would basically spend all day coding and living on Chinese food. He is a self-described hacker and created the GCC (the GNU Compiler Collection) which to this day is the underpinning of a huge percentage of the world's machine code and he also made EMACS (an early editor that was used to develop much of early software -- and even some to this day). By themselves, this would be impressive, perhaps even worth a mention on this list, but this is only the beginning of his legacy.

Perhaps most importantly, Richard Stallman developed the GNU Project, the GNU General Public License, and the Free Software Foundation. Collectively this was effectively a moral imperative (in Stallman's view): that all software should (if the world was truly ethical) be open/free. And that coders from all over should work on making free alternatives to proprietary software (the precursor to Linux). Free here does not mean free as in price, but free as in sharing/distribution/modification. Stallman disliked the term Open Source as it was a later invention to make "free software" more appealing to businesses as that has an unsurprisngly negative connatation in the corporate world.

Further cementing Stallman's legacy was when Linus Torvalds provided a working kernel (the working heart of an operating system: one of the only things Stallman's GNU Project lacked) which was licensed under Stallman's GPL, ensuring the Linux Kernel would remain "free" (by modern definitions: Open Source) for all time -- as the GPL enforces that no derivatives can be made unless also open sourced themsevles.  

To this day Richard Stallman holds some resentment that Linux has overshadowed GNU famously insisting Linux be called "GNU / Linux." Although widely mocked for this view, Stallman may have a valid point -- it was the GNU software AND community which he rallied that made Linux what it was, it is likely that both Stallman and Linus needed each other to accomplish what they did. In some ways I actually feel worse for Stallman than for Linus. Linus gets his (rightlyfully deserved) credit, whereas Stallman is often seen as the dinosaur who is trying to claim credit for things that he hasn't done, when in reality, I think he truly deserves credit as a visionary, akin to Ada Lovelace whose ideas were so insanely revolutionary people are still unraveling the full meaning. Even today some things he had said is seeming near prophetic with concerns over large technology corporations and privacy. And while Stallman, like Ada, may not have gotten all the facts right; I suspect even 200 years from now people will look back and say "That guy was surprisingly close to the mark which is absurd given how long ago he lived."

## Linus Torvalds (1991-2012\*)
*The first to develop a modern open source kernel --now the most used OS - ever (Android/Servers/Ubuntu)* 

Linus Torvalds, as many likely know, developed the Linux Kernel in 1991. There is little else to be said about this momentous achievement as much of it has been covered under the Stallman entry. It is important though to mention that the kernel was extremely difficult for Stallman and the GNU Project to create, theirs was called Hurd which only recently even became viable, and it is shocking how a young college kid shocked the industry and the world by developing a Kernel and proceeded to give it away for free. Even more shocking is that this one kernel, originally created by one man, is now used worldwide in everything from phones, to servers, to pcs.

Something else many people forget is that Linus also developed GIT, a hugely popular version management system for source code to this very day -- which directly impacts personal computers as most software developed uses GIT as part of the development process.

Linus still remains active, which is insane given how long he has been at it, however \* the date mentioned above (2012) is when he mostly stopped writing new code and transitioned into more of an oversight role doing merging and managing.
