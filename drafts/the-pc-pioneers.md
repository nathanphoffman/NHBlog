# The 9 True Pioneers of the PC
We have all heard the story that Jobs and Gates are responsible for the personal computer revolution. Maybe you have even heard of Gate's partner Paul Allen or Job's partner Steve Wozniak, who arguably 
made larger contributions (especially the former). There is no denying that without these guys, the personal computer would have been different -- but I surmise it still would exist. At the time they 
were hacking hardware and installing software, there were already dozens (if not hundreds) of hobbiests doing the same, they were not in anyway unique, they were just better business people and faster -- 
able to get the first to market.

To me, being lucky, opportunistic, and good at business does not make you a pioneer. Being a pioneer means you truly come up with a revolutionary idea, you lay a foundation upon which everything else 
is built. Even the iPhone, which arguably may be jobs lasting legacy, was not something he pioneered, he actually argued against turning the iPod into a phone saying

> Why the f*** would we want that? That is the dumbest idea I've ever heard.

Another problem is that many technologies we have came from corporations, or corporate funded labs like Bell Labs where ideas and innovators are hard to truly identify since it is all part of a shared 
funded team-framework. 

So here we root out teams and corporate funding (unless the person's contributions were beyond that of the team or corporation) and get to the root of the true pioneers of the PC.

## Charles Babbage, *1820-1840*
*The first to conceive of a general purpose machine with memory, a processor, and conditionals*

## Ada Lovelace, *1843*
*The first to envision that a general purpose machine does not need to be limited to mathematics* 

## George Boole, *1854*
*Created an entire branch of mathematics that would make the transistor and modern computers possible*

## Alan Turing, *1936-1950*
*The first to mathematically prove and demonstrate that a general purpose computer is possible*

## Claude Shannon, *1937-1948*
*Showed how bits and binary could be used to encode information -- the final pathway to electronic computers*

## Grace Hopper, *1952-1959*
*The first compiler and the first to champion readable code -- forsight that computers would extend to everyone*

## Gary Kildall, *1972-1991*
*The first to prove a PC was actually possibly by developing the 1st viable OS for a microprocessor*

Gary Kildall is (in my opinion) may not be the singular most important person in the history of computers (that likely goes to someone like Turing or Shannon) but he is very likely the singular most important person in the history of *Personal* computers. Gary Kildall provded PCs were viable before any computer clubs even toyed with the idea. He was likely the first (or one of the first) to even think that a microprocessor could run and compile high level programming languages and they could run an operating system, furthermore, he not only thought it, he was the first to demonstrate both in 1973 and again in 1974. Before Bill Gates was booting up a basic BASIC screen, Kildall had a fully functioning Operating System, his own programming language and compiler, and it all ran on his own microprocessor years before Bill ever got his working -- even before Wozniak got the Apple 1's basic non-kernel boot-stright-to-BASIC working -- hell before Wozniak even started theirs!  All of these guys knew of Gary and we know some of them like Gates even used and developed applications for CP/M.

So it is sad then that all these years later, no one knows of Gary Kildall. The truth is, Gary was, by all accounts, simply too nice. He was open with technological ideas, not trying to make a quick buck, and this likely led partially to why he wasn't willing to let IBM do whatever it wanted to his company (which is also what gave MSDOS its rise to dominance). But many people frame it as a bad business decision, no -- it was just a good human one. Gary cared about his employees and was a legitamate nerd, he cared more about that then money -- eventhough he ended up making a decent amount to be fair. But, he never achieved the fame or wealth of the others, and it is likely because of other's constant pestering of him and reminding him of his supposed failure that he turned to alcohol in his later years and ultimately died. Nothing sums up this unfairness better than this:

> In 1992, the University of Washington invited Kildall to attend the 25th anniversary of its computer science program. He was one of its earliest and most distinguished graduates, having earned a PhD there. They didn't ask him to address the audience despite his renown as a graduate. Instead they chose Bill Gates — who had dropped out of Harvard and never attended the University of Washington — to give the keynote, apparently because he had donated money. 



## Ed Roberts *1975*
*The first to see PC hardware as being economically producable for a home user*

Ed Roberts is arguably the weakest addition on this list, he does fall a bit under the lucky category, similar to that of jobs, gates, and others. However, he was truly the first to realize an economical PC was possible, even if his vision was not as grandoise as Gary Kildall. He saw the PC as a toy that could be manufactured to save his company that had sold rocket telemetry kits and calculators. When his calculator company crashed, he was forced to come up with new ideas, one of them was to sell a PC-like device as a toy with switches mounted on the front. It was a personal computer, just not a great one. Combined with Gary Kildalls vision of microprocessors as PCs and his software, and Ed Robert's hardware, the stage was set for the computer clubs and the famous folks everyone knows to tinker with these ideas and make commercially viable PCs.
